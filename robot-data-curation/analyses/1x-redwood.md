## 1. The data doctrine — the clearest mixture statement in the industry set

1X publishes an explicit ordered pipeline of data sources, presented as the recipe for autonomy:

> *"web-scale media + egocentric human videos + sim + dexterous remote operated robot data + on-policy NEO data → real-world deployment for robot data collection and RL → abundance of data → physical AI"*

And the moat claim, stated twice by two different people:

> *"We believe the only durable moat in embodied AI will be data: **the ability to collect, annotate, scale, and train on the right mixture** of web-scale, human, simulated, remote operator-generated robot data, and robot-generated data."*
> — 1X World Model Lab announcement

> *"We believe the only durable moat in AI is data — specifically, **the ability to collect and learn from the optimal mixture at large scales**. Embodied data has been treated as a second-class citizen for too long."*
> — Sam Sinha, Founding Researcher & Head of World Models

Note what is being claimed: not that more data wins, but that **the mixture** wins. That is the same variable GEN-0 A/B tests across data foundries, Qwen-VLA publishes as a proportion table, and ABot-M0 studies via sampling granularity. 1X asserts it as strategy without publishing the mixture.

## 2. "Robotics is not a fine-tuning problem"

The lab's founding thesis is a claim about *when* data must enter training:

> *"The lab is founded on a simple thesis: **robotics is not a fine-tuning problem**. To build truly general humanoids, we need to pretrain on the most important data from the very beginning. **Embodied intelligence requires models that see the physical world early, not just in post-training.** This includes video, ego-centric human experience, interaction data, simulation, robot data collected with remote operation, and on-policy NEO data. **Each provides a different signal about how the physical world works**."*

The methodological consequence, if taken seriously, is that mixture composition matters at pretraining rather than being correctable later — consistent with Qwen-RobotManip's finding that misaligned data simply fails to scale, and with GEN-0's finding that mixture determines model *character*, not just quality.

CEO Bernt Børnich adds the vertical-integration argument: *"to reach full autonomy fastest you must own the entire stack — down to pre-training your own video foundation models."*

## 3. The data flywheel — on-policy data as the distinguishing source

The one source in 1X's list that most competitors lack is **on-policy NEO data**: data generated by deployed robots executing the current policy.

> *"As 1X increases the amount of training compute and real world NEO data, the 1X World Model's accuracy of predicting whether NEO accomplishes a task also increases."*

Scale claim: **20,000 pre-ordered NEO humanoids shipping in 2026**, *"which will supply an ever-growing data flywheel"*, and *"with the NEO factory live, scaling the fleet to support real world data collection is **no longer the bottleneck**."*

> *"1X is uniquely positioned to connect the entire learning loop—from pretraining and robot learning to evaluation, deployment, and the **collection of on-policy data from real robots**. By closing this loop, the 1X World Model Lab will accelerate the development of increasingly capable foundation models."*

**Curation implication that 1X does not address:** on-policy data is, by construction, mixed-quality — it contains the current policy's failures. Every work in this survey that ingests autonomous rollouts has had to solve this explicitly: **π₀.₇** annotates quality/mistake metadata and conditions on it (and guards against eval leakage); **AGIBOT** retains and labels error-recovery trajectories; **π*₀.₆/RECAP** builds an RL objective around it. 1X states the flywheel but publishes nothing about how the loop is filtered — which is precisely the hard part.

## 4. World-model role
The 1X World Model is used for **policy evaluation** as well as pretraining — predicting whether NEO accomplishes a task. Using a learned world model as an evaluator is itself a data strategy: it converts expensive real-robot evaluation into cheap simulated evaluation, which in turn makes mixture ablations affordable.

## 5. What is not disclosed
- No corpus sizes for any of the five named sources.
- No mixture weights — despite mixture being the stated moat.
- No processing, filtering, annotation, or quality-control description.
- No handling of on-policy failure data.
- No scaling curves; the world-model accuracy claim is directional only.

## 6. Transferable takeaways
1. **Name your data sources as an ordered recipe.** Even without weights, the five-source doctrine (web media → ego human video → sim → teleop robot → on-policy robot) is a useful template for auditing a mixture's coverage.
2. **Each source contributes a different signal** — mixture design should be reasoned about in terms of what each source uniquely provides, not just its volume.
3. **Pretrain, don't fine-tune, on physical data** — if mixture composition is only fixable at pretraining, mixture decisions are the highest-leverage decisions.
4. **Deployment closes the loop**, but on-policy data arrives with failure baked in; the flywheel is only as good as the filter or conditioning scheme applied to it (see π₀.₇, AGIBOT).
5. **A learned world model is an evaluation instrument**, and cheap evaluation is what makes data-mixture science possible.
