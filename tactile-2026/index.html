Survey · 111 works · Jan–Aug 2026

Touch is easy to add and hard to use

2026 was the year tactile sensing arrived in robot foundation models — world models that predict contact, VLAs with dedicated tactile experts, cross-sensor pretraining at thousands of hours. It was also the year several groups measured, independently, that adding touch naively makes a policy worse than not having it at all, and that the benchmarks could not have told them either way. This is a reading of 111 works against one question: where does touch actually pay, and who checked?

The landscape

What the field means by “touch”

Before asking whether touch pays, it is worth asking what these 111 works are actually holding. Rows are how contact is transduced; columns are what the work builds on top of it. Every work sits in exactly one cell.

Touch, in 2026, is a camera pointed at a piece of rubber. Sixty of the 111 works sense contact through nothing but a camera watching a piece of rubber — a gel deforming in front of a fingertip lens, read out as an image — and most of the twenty-one cross-family works carry one among their sensors too. Taxel arrays and skins account for eighteen. Everything else — event cameras, contact microphones, bare proprioception — is four works combined. A modality defined by its bandwidth and its distribution over a body is being studied almost entirely through one transduction principle, mounted almost entirely on fingertips.

The architecture picked the sensor, not the other way round. Fourteen of the sixteen tactile world models use optical tactile, the tightest coupling in the grid. That is not a claim about which sensor carries the most contact information; it is a consequence of what the machinery can already predict. A world model has to generate the tactile future, and video-prediction backbones generate images. Commit to predicting touch and you have, in effect, committed to a sensor whose output is shaped like a frame.

The lighter the policy, the freer the sensor. Diffusion and action-chunking policies are the only row with no dominant sensor family — four cross-family, three arrays, three optical, one force-torque, one acoustic. They impose no requirement that touch look like anything in particular, so the sensor gets chosen on the task's merits. The two rows above them, VLA and world model, both inherit a pretrained backbone and both bend the sensor to fit it.

Twenty-nine works ship no model at all. The datasets, benchmarks, simulators and sensors are more than a quarter of the year's output, and the modelling works depend on them completely. Set against them, the sparsest corners of the grid are worth staring at: one work learns dexterous manipulation from binary contact flags and proprioception alone — and beats the previous state of the art on Baoding-ball rotation fourfold — and two works put no tactile sensor on the deployed robot at all, predicting the signal instead and matching the policies that measure it.

The year

Stars read from the GitHub API on 21 Aug 2026. A repository counts only if its README carries the paper's arXiv ID or its verbatim title, so same-name projects are excluded; 46 of the 111 works clear that bar and the other 65 are drawn at minimum size. Citation counts are not used — the corpus is Jan–Aug 2026 and OpenAlex returns zero for all 108 works it resolves.

Nearly a third of the year arrived in one month. Thirty-five of the 111 works were submitted in June, almost twice the next-busiest month — but at day resolution it is not a deadline spike. The submissions are spread across the whole month, two or three on most days, with only 30 June standing out at five. Whatever produced June was sustained rather than a single date, and it is worth knowing when reading the field: a large share of 2026's tactile literature was written to the same clock, which is part of why so many of these papers independently rediscovered the same negative result about naive fusion without citing each other.

Code is the minority position. Forty-six of the 111 works have a repository that can be verified as theirs; sixty-five released none that can be found. Among those that did, attention is narrow: the top four hold 271, 181, 173 and 170 stars, twenty-nine clear ten, nine sit in single digits and eight are still at zero. Two of the four best-starred are the same group's paired releases, N₀-TWAM and N₀-VTLA. Most of what this year produced is not yet running in anyone else's lab.

The sensing mix barely moves. Optical tactile is the plurality in six of the eight months, and the two exceptions are the thinnest — January with three works, May with seven. The sparse families stay sparse throughout: the year's one proprioception-only work lands in May, the three event and acoustic works are scattered across February, June and August. Whatever changed over these eight months, it was not what the field was willing to put on a fingertip.

Cross-cutting

Six things the field measured this year

Each of these was found independently by groups who were not arguing with each other — usually as a control inside a paper about something else.

01 Naive fusion is not neutral. It is destructive.

The clearest version is AT-VLA's table. Against a 0.22 vision-only baseline, injecting tactile straight into a pretrained VLA scores 0.13 with 6-D force, 0.05 with 2-D markers, and 0.02 with gel images. The failure mode is diagnosed precisely: most failures occur during object grasping, before contact, because tactile tokens displaced the pretrained visual grounding.

Tactile-WAM finds naive injection (0.151) below RGB-only (0.156). ForceVLA2 finds force in the VLM pathway collapses to 5%. TAP-VLA finds a conventional learned tactile encoder performs at chance on tasks its rendered-arrow version solves 80% of the time. MoSS finds three published methods degrade when given a second physical modality. FTP-1 finds Tactile-VLA scoring below vision-only π₀.₅ on unseen sensors.

02 Do not reconstruct raw tactile

Tactile-WAM sampled 5,000 tactile image pairs and found no clear correlation between pixel variation and contact variation. Pixel reconstruction therefore optimises the wrong thing — and a large share of 2026's tactile world models optimise exactly that.

Three other measurements agree from different directions. RATG: swapping a latent target for a raw one drops plug insertion 80% → 55%. Touch Dreaming: latent-space tactile prediction beats raw by 30% relative. TacGen: a pixel-space DDPM produces tactile-looking output with a 13-point downstream utility gap. ViTacWorld adds the corollary — tactile PSNR barely moves because most tactile frames are empty; only LPIPS sees the contact.

03 Where you put it beats what you put

DECO swaps which modality gets cross-attention and which gets AdaLN, changing nothing else, and the precision stage of two assembly tasks goes 29/40 → 0/40. ForceVLA2 tests three injection points and finds a 5% / 58% / 66% spread. RATG uses frozen linear probes to locate where future tactile is linearly accessible, moves one loss there, and gains 33 points — while applying the same loss at every layer scores below no tactile prediction at all.

The same shape appears in hardware. Tactile Genesis — a sensor-design sweep impossible in a real lab — finds placement dominates sensor type and that 200 taxels across the whole hand suffices, contradicting how commercial tactile fingertips are built. TacO finds no resolution effect on real manipulation success at all.

04 Touch is sparse, and that is the whole design problem

VT-WAM measured the effective contact ratio across six real tasks: 0.26–0.41. Between 59% and 74% of frames carry no informative tactile signal. ForceVLA2's skill decomposition finds 45.6% of a contact-rich industrial dataset is exploration. N₀-VTLA puts it plainly: tactile frames are "noisy, nearly empty away from contact, and informative almost only in the brief windows when contact forms."

That temporal imbalance is what biases gradients toward vision during joint training — and it is why every successful design here does something about it: a gate, a prior, a residual, a separate clock, or a different objective entirely.

05 The benchmarks could not have told you

RCT audited the released TVL/HCT split — the one much tactile-language work reports on — and found every test contact sequence also appears in training, with a training-free raw-pixel nearest neighbour recovering the correct sequence 98.3% of the time. Removing sequence overlap costs 17.7 points; holding out materials costs 42 more.

SoftVTBench shows continuous gripper control reproduces an entire apparent tactile gain, so any study not matching action spaces is crediting touch with finer actuation. TacO shows sensor rubber moves vision-only success by up to 20 points. TacVerse puts cross-sensor force transfer at R² = −9. The 3D-printable texture set shows a 99.5% classifier falling to 31% across printers, because it had learned print defects.

06 And the sensor may not need to be there

Six works report near-parity, or better, with no tactile input at deployment. HapticVLA distils tactile into a predicted token and beats baselines that keep the sensor. UniTacVLA's sensor-free variant is competitive. AT-VLA retains performance when tactile is absent. MiTaS gains over 10% from co-training with an event sensor the deployed policy never sees. UniTac's predicted-tactile VLA reaches 80% against 95% with the real sensor and 20% with neither. FELT's latent features match real tactile in the 60-demonstration regime.

Three papers independently offer the same explanation: predicted latents are smoother than real readings. FELT states it as a hypothesis and says so.

Making touch available does not by itself ensure effective multimodal fusion. SoftVTBench, arXiv:2608.18701
Three responses to one problem

What to do about a modality that is silent most of the time

Every working design in this survey answers the sparsity problem in one of three ways, and the choice determines what else the system can do.

Gate it

Let touch in only when there is contact

Cheapest, and it works. The disagreement is only over what computes the gate: a frame difference, a learned contact probability, visual uncertainty, or a written-out sentence.

  • Dream-Tac — non-learned frame-difference gate
  • FeelWorld — contact gates cross-attention
  • ResTacVLA — gate on visual uncertainty
  • CAAT — explicit prior beats a learned gate
  • UniTacVLA — the gate is a chain of thought
Predict it

Forecast the contact, then act on the forecast

Turns a reactive signal into an anticipatory one. But the forecast must be latent, not raw — and ReTouch shows it must be refreshed inside the action chunk, or it is worth 0.8 points.

  • N₀-TWAM — predicted and observed, two pathways
  • TacForeSight — wrist wrench predicts fingertip touch
  • ReTouch — refresh mid-chunk, worth 15.2 points
  • TacWAM — predict it, never let the actor read it
  • HiTac-WAM — rank candidate actions by it
Restructure around it

Change the rate, the space, or the objective

The most invasive answer and the most general. If touch cannot fit the policy's clock or coordinate system, change the policy rather than the signal.

  • TacMamba — 100 Hz history in O(1) state
  • TouchWorld — 1 / 10 / 30 Hz hierarchy
  • TDP — replace the action chunk with a tube
  • ForceVLA2 — put force in the action space
  • TAP-VLA · RGB-S — render touch into the image
Open question

Is the sensor the point, or is the supervision?

The field's implicit premise is that a deployed robot needs a tactile sensor. Almost every architecture here is built around delivering a live tactile signal to a policy at runtime. But the year's most awkward result is that several systems do nearly as well without one — and nobody has yet run the experiment that would settle it.

105

Works that assume the sensor is deployed

Gates, world models, cross-sensor token spaces, 30,000-hour corpora, foundation policies, wearable rigs, FPGA reflex loops. All of it presumes touch arrives at inference. TacVerse, HTT, UniForce, FTP-1 and TactX exist because different deployed sensors disagree with each other.

versus
6

Works that match their own sensored baseline without it

HapticVLA, UniTacVLA†, AT-VLA, MiTaS co-training, UniTac's VTLA-pred, FELT features. If the value of touch is a prior that can be baked in offline, tactile hardware is a data-collection cost rather than a deployment requirement — and the economics of the field change.

The two positions are not yet in contact. The sensor-free results all come from small-data regimes, on tasks where a learned prior may simply be enough; the sensored results come from settings where nobody tried removing the sensor. What is missing is the matched experiment: same task, same data scale, real tactile against predicted tactile, scored with the deformation-aware or safety-aware metric that SoftVTBench and TactiDex argue for. Because if predicted touch matches real touch on success but not on how hard the robot squeezed, the question answers itself.
